## SQ2 — How consistency across interaction types is achieved

### S2.1 NN/g — Design Systems 101 (Therese Fessenden, 11 Apr 2021) — **full**
URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/
- "A design system is a complete set of standards intended to manage design at scale using reusable components and patterns."
- "It creates a unified language within and between crossfunctional teams."
- "It creates visual consistency across products, channels, and (potentially siloed) departments."
- Style guides "contain specific implementation guidelines, visual references, and design principles for creating interfaces."
- "Creating and maintaining a design system is a time-intensive activity which requires a dedicated team."
- "A design system is only as effective as the team that manages it."
- Pitfall: design systems "can become unwieldly collections of components and code" when poorly implemented.
Relevance: the mechanism is standards + a *dedicated owning team*; consistency is explicitly cross-channel.

### S2.2 NN/g — Consistency and Standards (Heuristic 4) (Rachel Krause, 10 Jan 2021) — **full**
URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/consistency-and-standards/
- "Users should not have to wonder whether different words, situations, or actions mean the same thing."
- "Systems should adhere to both internal and external consistency — they should use the same patterns everywhere inside the system and should also follow web-, platform-, and domain-specific conventions."
- Jakob's law: "People spend most of their time on sites other than yours, which means that they bring their expectations from other sites when they visit your site."
- "Most application suites will list menu components in the same order or will use consistent icons for the same functions across applications."
- "Creating a design system for your application or family of applications can help teams maintain consistency across products and services."
- "Designing something against conventions will add to your users' cognitive load. You'll want to do this only if absolutely necessary."
- "In most cases, maintaining consistency and meeting user expectations will outweigh breaking a convention."
Relevance: gives the two-axis test (internal vs external consistency) — for a CLI, "external" means POSIX/GNU/terminal conventions (cf. clig.dev S3.1), not the GUI's conventions.

### S2.3 NN/g — Design Principles (Maria Rosala, 2 Aug 2020) — **full**
URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-principles/
- "Product design principles are value statements that frame design decisions and support consistency in decision making across teams."
- "These principles support consistency in the way decisions are being made across teams, build confidence in the decision, and eliminate fruitless debates."
- "Each principle should be clear on what value it advocates and why. If the principle is ambiguous about what it recommends, then it could be interpreted differently."
- "A design principle should mention why that value is important to users. By doing so it helps designers keep users at the heart of their design decisions."
- "When you make a trade-off decision, don't just say, 'we're doing it this way because I say so'...Say that we're doing it because principle #3 tips the balance in favor of one of the options."
- "Be careful that your principles don't conflict. For example, if there was a principle about consistency and another one about adaptability, then it would be difficult to understand how these values should be prioritized."
Relevance: principles as the decision instrument that carries consistency *across interaction types* where no shared component library can (CLI vs GUI vs API). Matches this shop's principle-set form.

### S2.4 NN/g — Seamless cross-channel / omnichannel UX (Kim Flaherty, 19 Mar 2017) — **full**
URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/seamless-cross-channel/
- "Users interact with companies and organizations through many channels, including the web, mobile and tablet applications, email, kiosks, online chat, and by speaking with customer representatives."
- "Often users don't complete an activity in one sitting or through a single channel."
- "Seamlessness is a quality of any crosschannel customer journey where the transitions (or handoffs) from one channel to the next involve zero or minimal overhead for the users."
- "Five key elements of a usable omnichannel experience: Consistent, Optimized for context, Seamless, Orchestrated, and Collaborative."
- "A truly seamless experience often requires investment in integrating backend systems to prevent roadblocks stemming back to system constraints."
- "If the interaction cost of doing an activity overcomes its perceived value, users will abandon the task or the brand."
Relevance: "Consistent" and "Optimized for context" are listed side by side — consistency is not sameness (cf. GOV.UK "consistent, not uniform"). Handoffs between interaction types (CLI → browser, cf. GitHub CLI S3.4) are a design object in their own right.

### S2.5 NN/g — Four Dimensions of Tone of Voice (Kate Moran, Jul 2016) — **full**
URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions/
- Dimensions: "Formal vs. casual, serious vs. funny, respectful vs. irreverent, matter-of-fact vs. enthusiastic."
- "Tone of voice is the way we tell our users how we feel about our message, and it will influence how they'll feel about our message, too."
- "Keep your brand personality consistent, but vary the tone to fit the user's emotional state and the topic."
- "Your tone of voice should be part of your omnichannel strategy... used when creating writing and other communications for all channels."
- "All members of the digital product team need to think about how their piece fits within the whole."
Relevance: voice is an interaction-type-independent consistency carrier (error messages in a CLI, API error strings, chat replies).

### S2.6 GOV.UK — Government Design Principles — **full**
URL: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles
- 1 Start with user needs: "Service design starts with identifying user needs. If you don't know what the user needs are, you won't build the right thing."
- 3 Design with data: "Let data drive decision-making, not hunches or guesswork. Keep doing that after taking your service live, prototyping and testing with users."
- 4 Do the hard work to make it simple: "Making something look simple is easy. Making something simple to use is much harder - especially when the underlying systems are complex."
- 6 This is for everyone: "Accessible design is good design. Everything we build should be as inclusive, legible and readable as possible."
- 7 Understand context: "We're not designing for a screen, we're designing for people. We need to think hard about the context in which they're using our services."
- 9 Be consistent, not uniform: "We should use the same language and the same design patterns wherever possible. This isn't a straitjacket or a rule book."
- 10 Make things open: "We should share what we're doing whenever we can. With colleagues, with users, with the world. Share code, share designs, share ideas."
Relevance: an external, mature principle set; #9 is the canonical phrasing of cross-interaction-type consistency; #7 justifies per-interaction-type optimisation.

### S2.7 GOV.UK Design System — Contribution criteria — **full**
URL: https://design-system.service.gov.uk/community/contribution-criteria/
- Proposal — useful: "There is evidence that this component or pattern would be useful for many teams or services."
- Proposal — unique: "It does not replicate something already in the Design System."
- Publication — usable: "It has been tested in user research and shown to work with a representative sample of users, including those with disabilities."
- Publication — consistent: "It reuses existing styles and components in the Design System where relevant."
- Publication — "Both the guidance and any content included in examples must follow the GOV.UK style guide."
- Publication — versatile: "The implementation is versatile enough that the component or pattern can be used in a range of different services."
- Community — "The component or pattern must also have been tested and shown to work with a range of browsers, assistive technologies and devices."; "it has a clear owner".
Relevance: a design system's governance is a *gate with stated criteria* (usefulness, uniqueness, evidence of usability incl. disabled users, consistency, ownership) — directly reusable as a fitness set.

## SQ3 — CLI and developer-facing UX as design

### S3.1 clig.dev — Command Line Interface Guidelines (Aanand Prasad, Ben Firshman, Carl Tashian, Eva Parish) — **full**
URL: https://clig.dev/
- "If a command is going to be used primarily by humans, it should be designed for humans first."
- "Whatever software you're building, people will use it in ways you didn't anticipate. Your software will become a part in a larger system."
- Consistency: "The terminal's conventions are hardwired into our fingers...that's what makes CLIs intuitive and guessable; that's what makes users efficient."
- "The efficiency of using the command-line comes from remembering commands, but there's no reason the commands can't help you learn."
- "Beyond the most utterly simple commands, running a program usually involves more than one invocation...like a conversation the user is having with the program."
- "Software should be robust: unexpected input should be handled gracefully, operations should be idempotent where possible."
- "Command-line tools are a programmer's creative toolkit, so they should be enjoyable to use...delighting the user means exceeding their expectations."
- "Human-readable output is paramount. Humans come first, machines second...whether an output stream is being read by a human is whether or not it's a TTY."
- "Catch errors and rewrite them for humans...the user has done something wrong and the program is guiding them in the right direction."
- "Prefer flags to args...it makes much clearer what is going on and easier to make changes to how you accept input."
- "Only use prompts or interactive elements if stdin is an interactive terminal (a TTY)...a prompt won't work in a script."
- On GUI vs CLI: "The original Macintosh Human Interface Guidelines recommend 'See-and-point (instead of remember-and-type),' as if you could only choose one or the other. These things needn't be mutually exclusive."
Relevance: the most complete public CLI design guideline; explicitly frames CLI as *design* borrowing GUI concepts (discoverability, conversation) while honouring terminal conventions (external consistency, cf. S2.2).

### S3.2 Heroku Dev Center — CLI Style Guide — **full**
URL: https://devcenter.heroku.com/articles/cli-style-guide
- "Heroku CLI plugins should provide a clear user experience, targeted primarily for human readability and usability, which delights the user, while at the same time supporting advanced users and output formats."
- "The Heroku CLI is for humans before machines. The primary goal of anyone developing CLI plugins should always be usability."
- "Input and output should be consistent across commands to allow the user to easily learn how to interact with new commands."
- "Flags are preferred to args. They involve a bit more typing, but make the use of the CLI clearer."
- "However, if prompting is required to complete a command, this means the user will not be able to script the command. Ensure that args or flags can always be provided to bypass the prompt."
- "Human-readable output should be grep-parseable, but not necessarily awk-parseable."
- "Terse, machine-readable output formats can also be useful but shouldn't get in the way of making beautiful CLI output. When needed, commands should offer a `--json` and/or a `--terse` flag when valuable."
- "Stdout should be used for all output and stderr for warning, errors and out of band information..."
- "Topic and command descriptions should be provided for all topics and commands. They should fit on 80 character width screens, begin with a lowercase character, and should not end in a period."

URLs: https://medium.com/@jdxcode/12-factor-cli-apps-dd3c227a0e46 (403) ; mirror opened in full: https://panlw.github.io/15394417631263.html (English original text)

### S3.4 GitHub — Primer CLI design guidelines (archived repo primer/cli; live site primer.style/cli redirects to the archived repo) — **full (source .mdx read via `gh api`)**
URLs: https://primer.style/cli/ (308 → https://github.com/primer/cli/, archived 3 Jul 2024, "This repo is not maintained anymore. Please raise pull requests in primer.style/design"); content files read: content/getting-started/principles.mdx, process.mdx, foundations/index.mdx, language.mdx, scriptability.mdx. - Principle "Reasonable defaults, easy overrides": "Optimize for what most people will need to do most of the time, but make it easy for people to adjust it to their needs."
- Principle "Make it feel like GitHub": "Using this tool, it should be obvious that it's GitHub and not anything else. Use details that are specific to GitHub, such as language or color. When designing output, reflect the GitHub.com interface as much as possible and appropriate."
- Principle "Reduce cognitive load": "Command line interfaces are not as visually intuitive as graphical interfaces. - Principle "Bias towards terminal, but make it easy to get to the browser": "We want to help people stay in the terminal wherever they might want to maintain focus and reduce context switching, but when it's necessary to jump to GitHub.com make it obvious, fast, and easy. Certain actions are probably better to do in a visual interface."
- Foundations: "These are the foundational design concepts and constraints that can help inform design decisions."
- Language: "Language is the most important tool at our disposal for creating a clear, understandable product. Having clear language helps us create memorable commands that are clear in what they will do."
- Scriptability: "Make choices that ensure that creating automations or scripts with GitHub commands is obvious and frictionless. - Process (design questions per command): "What makes sense to do from a terminal? Relevance: the clearest example of a *design-system treatment of a CLI* (principles, foundations, components) and of cross-interaction-type consistency as a stated principle ("make it feel like GitHub", handoff to browser). Note it lives under Primer, GitHub's design system — i.e. CLI design is owned by the design-system function.

## Cross-source conflicts and observations

1. **Consistency vs context.** GOV.UK #9 ("consistent, not uniform"), #7 ("understand context"), NN/g omnichannel ("Consistent" and "Optimized for context" as separate elements), and Primer CLI ("Certain actions are probably better to do in a visual interface") all agree that cross-interaction-type consistency is of language, patterns and identity, not of form. NN/g heuristic 4 adds the external axis: each interaction type has its own platform conventions (terminal conventions per clig.dev; HTTP/REST per Azure) that must also be honoured. No source claims a single component library can span CLI/GUI/API.
2. **What carries consistency across interaction types**, per the opened sources: (a) principles (NN/g Rosala; GOV.UK; Primer CLI principles), (b) language/vocabulary and voice (Primer "Make it feel like GitHub"/Language; NN/g tone; GOV.UK style guide; Heroku naming rules), (c) tokens as single source of truth (Atlassian), (d) accessibility standard (WCAG 2.2 AA; WCAG2ICT for non-web), (e) a governed guidance corpus with approvers and criteria (GOV.UK contribution criteria and working group; Google AIP editors/approvers; Azure Stewardship Board).
3. **Governance form is the same across design systems and API guidelines**: criteria + review body + approval state + reviewers citing the corpus.
