    15	# Experience principles
    16	
    17	## What this set governs
    18	
    19	This is the experience-scope principle set: the standing rules for how
    20	the product's interactions are designed, whichever interaction type a
    21	person or an agent reaches the product by — command line, terminal,
    22	graphical or web, API and SDK, conversational, voice, or a generated
    23	document. It is a peer of the working-scope set,
    24	[Founding principles](principles.md), which governs how every activity
    25	is performed, and of the architecture-scope set,
    26	[Architecture principles](architecture-principles.md), which governs
    27	what the designed system must look like; this set governs what a person
    28	or an agent meets when they use it. It is the first layer of the
    29	experience guidance corpus the
    30	[product designer role](roles/lead-product-designer.md) owns; the
    31	guidelines that apply it to each interaction type sit beneath it.
    32	Backticked slugs cite principles; a slug not defined in this document is
    33	defined in the working or architecture set. The terms interaction type,
    34	experience guidance corpus, generated interface, BC-shop, contract, and
    35	intent are defined in the [glossary](glossary.md). Two words carry one
    36	sense each here: an *agent* is a software caller using the product on
    37	someone's behalf; an *assistant interaction* is one in which the
    38	product itself acts on an intent the person stated rather than on a
    39	command — conversational, voice, or an assistant acting for the person
    40	(a closed set; the solutions architect role is the lead shop's
    41	architecture role). Where a principle here applies another set's
    42	principle at the experience level, the frontmatter declares the lineage
    43	in `derives-from` instead of restating the rule as a second authority.
    44	
    45	## What a good principle looks like
    46	
    47	This section restates the shared definition from the
    48	[principle-set typedef](artifacts/principle-set.md) so this document can
    49	be read alone; the restatement is a rendering, not a second authority.
    50	
    51	A principle is a standing rule, in four parts: a name, a statement, a
    52	rationale, and implications.
    53	
    54	- The **statement** is the rule. It carries the only normative keywords
    55	  (MUST, SHOULD, MAY — interpreted per BCP 14 when, and only when, they
    56	  appear in capitals) and is testable: shown a piece of work, you can
    57	  answer yes or no. A statement carrying more than one obligation
    58	  presents one obligation per bullet.
    59	- The **rationale** says why the rule earns its place: the failure it
    60	  prevents, shown as a generic example; well-known external references
    61	  may support it, the product's own history never appears. Rationales
    62	  stay prose.
    63	- The **implications** are the price tag: one implication per bullet,
    64	  the concrete change each named actor absorbs to honor the rule. They
    65	  add no obligations — every implication must be derivable from the
    66	  statement.
    67	
    68	A good principle passes the tests applied in the fitness screen at the
    69	end: its statement is testable (TOGAF's understandable, complete,
    70	consistent); it helps you say no (Jared Spool, "Creating Great Design
    71	Principles"); it is not fluff and not a goal in disguise (Richard
    72	Rumelt, *Good Strategy/Bad Strategy*); it is not permission-to-play —
    73	something every competent product already does (Patrick Lencioni, "Make
    74	Your Values Mean Something"); and it implies at least one practice and
    75	at least one check.
    76	
    77	## The principles
    78	
    79	### Consistent, not uniform (`consistent-not-uniform`)
    80	
    81	**Statement.**
    82	- Every interaction type MUST name the product's things and actions with
    83	  the product's one vocabulary, as the experience guidance corpus
    84	  records it.
    85	- Every interaction type MUST follow, for its form and idiom, the
    86	  platform interface guidelines the corpus names for that type.
    87	- Where a platform guideline names an action differently from the
    88	  vocabulary, the platform's word MUST be used in that interaction
    89	  type and the mapping recorded in the vocabulary.
    90	- A rule of the corpus MAY be varied for one interaction type only
    91	  with the variation and its reason recorded in the corpus.
    92	
    93	**Rationale.** A product reached through a command line, a web page,
    94	and a chat window is three products when each names the same object
    95	differently, and a foreign one when its command line ignores the
    96	terminal's conventions to imitate its web page. People carry their
    97	expectations from the platform they are on — Nielsen Norman Group's
    98	usability heuristic "consistency and standards" and Jakob's law of
    99	internet user experience — and from the last place they met the
   100	product. The GOV.UK design principles put it as "be consistent, not
   101	uniform": the same language and patterns wherever possible, without a
   102	straitjacket. Words and rules are shared; form follows the platform.
   103	
   104	**Implications.**
   105	- The product designer role maintains the vocabulary, its per-platform
   106	  mappings, and the named platform guidelines per interaction type in
   107	  the corpus.
   108	- The product designer role screens every interaction type's text
   109	  against the vocabulary.
   110	- BC-shops building an interaction cite the platform guideline the
   111	  corpus names for its type.
   112	- The product designer role records each variation with its reason; an
   113	  unrecorded variation is a finding against the corpus.
   114	
   115	### Core-task parity (`core-task-parity`)
   116	
   117	**Statement.**
   118	- Every interaction type the product offers MUST support every task on
   119	  the corpus's core-task list.
   120	- An option the product offers in one interaction type MUST be
   121	  reachable in every other interaction type that presents the same
   122	  task.
   123	
   124	**Rationale.** A voice assistant that can start an order but only a
   125	screen can confirm it strands the person who has no screen; a chat
   126	interface that offers three choices only one of which the graphical
   127	interface shows teaches people that the product hides things. Google's
   128	conversation design guidelines and Amazon's Alexa design guide converge
   129	on the rule: voice-first but not voice-only, every option offered in
   130	one modality reachable in the other, the core message the same across
   131	them. Parity of tasks and options is what lets a person move between
   132	interaction types without relearning what the product can do.
   133	
   134	**Implications.**
   135	- The product designer role keeps the core-task list in the corpus.
   136	- The product designer role screens each new interaction type against
   137	  the list before delivery.
   138	- BC-shops that add an option to one interaction type add it to every
   139	  other type presenting that task.
   140	- The PO role's acceptance scenarios for a task name the interaction
   141	  types it must hold on.
   142	
   143	### The agent is a user (`agent-is-a-user`)
   144	
   145	**Statement.**
   146	- Every interface an agent uses to reach the product — a tool
   147	  definition, a command line, an API, or an SDK (a closed set) — MUST
   148	  be designed as an interface: named for its caller, its behavior
   149	  stated in its documentation, and screened by the product designer
   150	  role.
   151	- An agent-facing interface MUST NOT expose a capability a person could
   152	  not reach through some interaction type.
   153	
   154	**Rationale.** A tool whose parameters are named for the implementer
   155	rather than the caller is misread by the agent calling it, and the
   156	agent's failure is recorded as the agent's. Nielsen Norman Group's "AI
   157	Agents as Users" states the shift plainly — "user" is no longer
   158	synonymous with "human" — and Anthropic's guidance on building agents
   159	asks for as much care in the agent-computer interface as in the human
   160	one. This principle is `actor-neutral-discipline` applied at the
   161	interface: the discipline of design does not lapse because the user is
   162	an agent, and an agent gains no capability a person lacks. An interface
   163	no one designed is still an interface; it is only an undesigned one.
   164	
   165	**Implications.**
   166	- The solutions architect role submits contracts, command lines, and
   167	  tool definitions to the product designer role's screen before they
   168	  are published.
   169	- BC-shops write tool and API documentation for the caller, with the
   170	  same care as user-facing help.
   171	- The product designer role screens agent-facing interfaces against
   172	  the corpus with the same patterns as human-facing ones.
   173	
   174	### Evidence, not opinion (`evidence-not-opinion`)
   175	
   176	**Statement.**
   177	- A claim that an interaction is usable MUST rest on observed use — a
   178	  user test, a tested prototype, or measured task completion (a closed
   179	  set the corpus may extend) — or be labeled a hypothesis.
   180	- A preference — a stakeholder's, a maker's, the designer's own — MUST
   181	  NOT decide a design question that observed use can answer; what the
   182	  product is for is the PM role's decision, not a design question.
   183	
   184	**Rationale.** Design by committee, where every opinion in the room
   185	weighs the same, produces the least-common-denominator interface; the
   186	alternative is not one taste in charge but one decision-maker deciding
   187	on evidence. Human-centred design as ISO 9241-210 describes it is
   188	driven and refined by user-centred evaluation; GOV.UK's third design
   189	principle is "design with data." Nielsen Norman Group's "The
   190	Product-Manager Archetype" names the failure the second bullet
   191	prevents: a sketch handed over as a requirement, with the designer
   192	asked to polish it instead of to test it.
   193	
   194	**Implications.**
   195	- The product designer role records the evidence behind each design
   196	  decision in the corpus, and labels the rest hypotheses.
   197	- The PM role treats a wireframe or sketch from any role as input to be
   198	  tested, never as a requirement.
   199	- BC-shops label an untested interaction's usability a hypothesis when
   200	  they deliver it.
   201	
   202	### Accessible by standard (`accessible-by-standard`)
   203	
   204	**Statement.**
   205	- Every graphical, web, and document interaction MUST meet WCAG 2.2 at
   206	  level AA.
   207	- Every non-web interaction — command line, terminal, voice, and
   208	  assistant interactions (a closed set; APIs and SDKs are agent-facing
   209	  and governed by `agent-is-a-user`) — MUST apply the WCAG 2.2
   210	  success criteria as WCAG2ICT describes for non-web software, with a
   211	  record of which criteria do not apply and why.
   212	- Accessibility conformance MUST be established before an interaction
   213	  is delivered.
   214	
   215	**Rationale.** An interface that conveys meaning by color alone, or a
   216	terminal screen that a screen reader cannot follow, excludes the people
   217	who depend on assistive technology and, increasingly, the agents that
   218	read the same structure. The W3C's guidelines are the one shared
   219	standard — WCAG 2.2 is ISO/IEC 40500:2025 — and, as GOV.UK's sixth
   220	design principle has it, accessible design is good design: the
   221	discipline it imposes, clear names and predictable structure, serves
   222	every user. Retrofitting accessibility costs more than building it and
   223	reaches fewer.
   224	
   225	**Implications.**
   226	- The product designer role names the accessibility target per
   227	  interaction type in the corpus, with the WCAG2ICT applicability
   228	  record for non-web types.
   229	- BC-shops test each interaction with assistive technology before
   230	  delivery and attach the result.
   231	- The PO role's acceptance scenarios for an interaction include its
   232	  accessibility criteria.
   233	
   234	### Errors guide recovery (`errors-guide-recovery`)
   235	
   236	**Statement.**
   237	- Every error the product shows MUST say what happened, in the
   238	  product's vocabulary and the person's natural language, and what the
   239	  person can do next.
   240	- An error MUST NOT expose an internal identifier or message in place
   241	  of that explanation, though it MAY carry one alongside it.
   242	
   243	**Rationale.** A command that fails with a stack trace, a form that
   244	says "invalid input" without saying which field, a chat assistant that
   245	answers a misunderstanding with silence — each leaves the person to
   246	guess, and in a command line, where there is no interface to guide
   247	them, the error message is the interface. The Command Line Interface
   248	Guidelines (clig.dev) say it directly: catch errors and rewrite them
   249	for humans. Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction ask the
   250	same of assistants — support efficient correction — and Google's
   251	conversation design guidelines warn that one poorly handled error
   252	outweighs dozens of successful turns.
   253	
   254	**Implications.**
   255	- BC-shops write every user-facing error with its next step, and keep
   256	  the internal detail in a log or an alongside field.
   257	- The product designer role screens error text against the vocabulary
   258	  and voice.
   259	- The solutions architect role's contracts define error responses that
   260	  carry a human-readable explanation, not only a code.
   261	
   262	### Control stays with the person (`control-stays-with-the-person`)
   263	
   264	**Statement.**
   265	- An assistant interaction MUST make clear what the product can do.
   266	- An assistant interaction MUST say what it is about to do before an
   267	  action the corpus classes as hard to reverse.
   268	- An assistant interaction MUST let the person correct, dismiss, or
   269	  stop it at any point.
   270	- An assistant interaction MUST hand control back to the person when it
   271	  fails.
   272	- A generated interface MUST stay within the constraints the corpus
   273	  states for it; it MAY vary its form within them.
   274	
   275	**Rationale.** Intent-based interaction reverses the locus of control:
   276	the person states an outcome and the system chooses the steps. That is
   277	its value and its danger. An assistant that books the wrong thing
   278	silently, or cannot be interrupted, or shows a different interface each
   279	visit, loses the trust that makes delegation possible. Microsoft's
   280	Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction — make clear what the system can
   281	do, support efficient invocation, dismissal, and correction, provide
   282	global controls — and Ben Shneiderman's *Human-Centered AI*, which
   283	holds that high automation and high human control are compatible
   284	rather than a trade-off, point the same way: the system may act, and
   285	the person stays in charge.
   286	
   287	**Implications.**
   288	- BC-shops building an assistant interaction implement the confirm,
   289	  correct, dismiss, and stop controls before the interaction is
   290	  delivered.
   291	- The product designer role screens assistant interactions against the
   292	  human-AI guidelines named in the corpus.
   293	- The product designer role states, in the corpus, which actions are
   294	  hard to reverse and the constraints a generated interface varies
   295	  within.

    14	# Guideline: every interaction
    15	
    16	**Voice principle.** Design every interaction for the person or agent
    17	who arrives from another part of the product and expects the same
    18	words, the same tasks, an error that helps, and an interface they can
    19	use — whichever way they reached it.
    20	
    21	**Highlights (the layer compiled into generating context):** the
    22	vocabulary's word, or a recorded platform mapping · the platform's
    23	idiom for form, departures recorded · every error: what happened, in
    24	the vocabulary, and what next; identifiers alongside · every core task
    25	and every option offered elsewhere · the accessibility target met and
    26	attached before delivery.
    27	
    28	**Layers:** this guideline applies the
    29	[experience principles](../experience-principles.md) to every
    30	interaction type; the per-type experience guidelines (command line,
    31	API, graphical, assistant, document) layer their idiom on it and never
    32	override it, as every prose guideline layers on the
    33	[base writing style](base-writing-style.md), which governs every
    34	string an interaction shows. Precedence when rules conflict: an
    35	approved principle beats the
    36	[quality-guideline typedef](../artifacts/quality-guideline.md), which
    37	beats this guideline, which beats a per-type guideline; the platform
    38	guideline the corpus names for a type governs that type's form, and
    39	the platform's word governs a name where `consistent-not-uniform`
    40	bullet 3 says so. Every rule feeds the
    41	[interaction fitness set](../fitness/interaction.fitness.md), judged by
    42	the product designer role. The rules here and in the per-type
    43	guidelines judge against corpus records the product designer role has
    44	not yet authored — the vocabulary and its platform mappings, the
    45	variations record, the core-task list, the interaction patterns, the
    46	design tokens, the hard-to-reverse classification, the persona and
    47	voice; none is yet filed as a work item. Until a record exists, a
    48	rule that needs it returns "undecidable: record absent" — a finding
    49	against the corpus, never a pass — as the fitness set states.
    50	
    51	---
    52	
    53	## Rules
    54	
    55	**1. The vocabulary's word, or a recorded platform mapping.**
    56	Before: the web interface says "Remove", the command is `delete`, the
    57	assistant says "drop".
    58	After: the vocabulary names the action once; each interaction type uses
    59	that name unless the platform guideline the corpus names for it uses
    60	another, and the mapping is recorded in the vocabulary.
    61	*Test:* list every noun and verb the interaction shows or accepts
    62	against the vocabulary and its recorded mappings. *Criterion:* every
    63	term is the vocabulary's or a recorded mapping. *Decision:* yes/no per
    64	interaction.
    65	*Derived check:* judged — interaction fitness scenario 1;
    66	`consistent-not-uniform` bullets 1 and 3.
    67	
    68	**2. The platform's idiom for form; departures recorded.**
    69	Before: a command line whose subcommands mimic the web navigation; a
    70	native app whose dialogs follow the web page instead of the operating
    71	system.
    72	After: form follows the platform guideline the corpus names for the
    73	type; the product designer role records a departure in the corpus
    74	with its reason.
    75	*Test:* compare the interaction's form with the named platform
    76	guideline. *Criterion:* every departure is a recorded variation.
    77	*Decision:* yes/no per interaction.
    78	*Derived check:* judged — interaction fitness scenario 2;
    79	`consistent-not-uniform` bullets 2 and 4.
    80	
    81	**3. Every error: what happened, in the vocabulary, and what next.**
    82	Before: `Error: ENOENT`; a red banner "Invalid input"; "Sorry, I didn't
    83	get that."
    84	After: the thing named in the vocabulary, the cause, and at least one
    85	next step; an internal identifier alongside — in a log, a verbose
    86	mode, or a field beside the message — never in place.
    87	*Test:* trigger each error path. *Criterion:* the message names what
    88	happened in the vocabulary and gives a next step; identifiers stand
    89	alongside only. *Decision:* yes/no per error.
    90	*Derived check:* judged — interaction fitness scenario 3;
    91	`errors-guide-recovery` bullets 1 and 2. Per-type guidelines say where
    92	the message goes (at the field; in the response body; as a repair
    93	turn — the assistant guideline's rule 5).
    94	
    95	**4. Every core task, and every option offered elsewhere.**
    96	Before: an export only the command line offers; a chat assistant with
    97	three choices of which the screen shows one.
    98	After: every task on the corpus's core-task list completes in the
    99	interaction, and every option another interaction type offers for the
   100	task is present.
   101	*Test:* walk the core-task list and the options other types offer.
   102	*Criterion:* every task on the list completes; every option is
   103	present. A task the type cannot carry is removed from the list for
   104	that type by the product designer role, with the reason recorded — the
   105	principle's own implication — never waived here.
   106	*Decision:* yes/no per interaction.
   107	*Derived check:* judged — interaction fitness scenario 4;
   108	`core-task-parity` bullets 1 and 2.
   109	
   110	**5. The accessibility target, met and attached, before delivery.**
   111	Before: an interaction delivered with accessibility "to follow".
   112	After: the delivery carries the accessibility result at the target the
   113	corpus names for the type — WCAG 2.2 AA for web, graphical, and
   114	document types; the WCAG2ICT application with its applicability record
   115	for command line, terminal, voice, and assistant types.
   116	*Test:* read the delivery's attached accessibility result. *Criterion:*
   117	it exists, meets the target, and carries the applicability record for
   118	a non-web type; the BC-shop that delivered the interaction attached it.
   119	*Decision:* yes/no per delivery.
   120	*Derived check:* judged — interaction fitness scenario 5;
   121	`accessible-by-standard` bullets 1–3.

    16	# Fitness set: interaction
    17	
    18	These scenarios are the conformance screen the product designer role
    19	runs on a delivered interaction of any interaction type against the
    20	experience guidance corpus — the [experience principles](../experience-principles.md),
    21	the [common experience guideline](../guidelines/experience-common.md),
    22	and the per-type guideline for the interaction's type. They are judged
    23	by the `lead-product-designer` role, never executed. The judge's model
    24	and prompt version (or the person's name) are recorded with each
    25	verdict. The process that runs this screen at delivery is not yet
    26	defined; until it is, the product designer role runs it on request from
    27	the [PO output check](../processes/po-output-check.md) or a Bounded
    28	Context shop; the process is not yet filed as a work item. Where a
    29	scenario judges against a corpus record that does not yet exist — the
    30	vocabulary, the variations record, the core-task list, the interaction
    31	patterns — the verdict is "undecidable: record absent", recorded as a
    32	finding against the corpus, never a pass.
    33	
    34	## Scenarios
    35	
    36	Scenario 1: one vocabulary, recorded mappings
    37	  Given every noun and verb the interaction shows or accepts
    38	  When each is compared with the corpus vocabulary
    39	  Then it is the vocabulary's word or a platform mapping the vocabulary
    40	  records
    41	
    42	Scenario 2: the platform's idiom
    43	  Given the platform guideline the corpus names for the interaction
    44	  type
    45	  When the interaction's form is compared with it
    46	  Then each departure is a variation the corpus records with a reason
    47	
    48	Scenario 3: errors guide recovery
    49	  Given each error the interaction can show
    50	  When it is read
    51	  Then it says what happened in the vocabulary and what to do next,
    52	  and any internal identifier stands alongside, not in place
    53	
    54	Scenario 4: core tasks and options hold
    55	  Given the corpus's core-task list and the options other interaction
    56	  types offer for those tasks
    57	  When the interaction is walked
    58	  Then every core task completes and every option offered elsewhere for
    59	  the task is present
    60	
    61	Scenario 5: accessible before delivery
    62	  Given the accessibility target the corpus names for the interaction
    63	  type
    64	  When the delivery's attached accessibility result is read
    65	  Then it exists, meets the target, and for a non-web type carries the
    66	  WCAG2ICT applicability record
    67	
    68	Scenario 6: the per-type rules hold
    69	  Given the per-type experience guideline for the interaction's type
    70	  When each of its rules' tests is run
    71	  Then each rule's criterion is met

    12	# Glossary
    13	
    14	## How the list combines
    15	
    16	The defined-term list is this glossary combined with every schema element
    17	name. Per the `use-defined-terms` principle, a writer choosing between
    18	terms uses one of these when one fits.
    19	
    20	## Terms
    21	
    22	- **artifact type** — a named, schema-defined document type (e.g.
    23	  `decision-brief`); the term the schema registry uses. Not "kind".
    24	- **data type** — a named, schema-defined structure that is not a
    25	  human-readable document (e.g. `review`); passed between process steps.
    26	- **simple type** — a JSON Schema primitive (`string`, `integer`,
    27	  `boolean`, `array`, `object`), usable inline without registration.
    28	- **typedef** — the single source a type is generated from; templates and
    29	  schema fragments are its renderings (owned by shopsystem-knowledge).
    30	- **schema** — the machine-checkable shape of a type: fields, types,
    31	  enums, required sections.
    32	- **process definition** — the source of truth for a process: header
    33	  (purpose, outcomes, roles), data section, steps section.
    34	- **step** — one unit of a process: an agent step (carries a prompt) or a
    35	  runtime step (carries `set`, `run`, or `branches`; no prose).
    36	- **rendering** — a generated output of a definition (a skill, a
    37	  diagram, a template); never edited by hand, never the source of truth.
    38	- **guiding statement** — an optional header element of a process
    39	  definition that directs judgment across the whole process; compiled
    40	  into every rendering.
    41	- **fitness set** — judged (never executed) Given/When/Then scenarios
    42	  scoring an artifact type's quality.
    43	- **guideline** — prose quality rules for an artifact type, each with a
    44	  test, a criterion, and a yes/no decision.
    45	- **role** — a named capability contract and set of accountabilities,
    46	  assigned by process steps to whoever fills it; sequencing lives in
    47	  process definitions, not roles.
    48	- **principle** — a standing rule about how we work: name, statement,
    49	  rationale, implications (see the principles document's opening
    50	  definition).
    51	- **seed layer** — the hand-approved definitions the regress terminates
    52	  at: the principle set plus one typedef per definition document type.
    53	- **owner** — the role that approves changes to a definition; named in
    54	  every definition's frontmatter.
    55	- **definition chain** — the six linked definitions of good for one
    56	  artifact type; required before instances are authored or rewritten.
    57	- **keeper** — a corpus record whose action is keep-rewrite; rewritten
    58	  forward through its type's approved chain, never used as-is.
    59	- **rewrite-forward** — the default for keepers: re-author to the
    60	  approved standard; repair-in-place only for records already at the bar.
    61	- **rebaseline** — Phase 1 of the re-founding arc: the active tree comes
    62	  to hold only chain-conforming records; retired mass moves to the
    63	  archive.
    64	- **scope** (of a principle set) — the level the set governs and whose
    65	  context it loads into: working (every activity), architecture (the
    66	  designed system), or experience (the product's interactions).
    67	- **transcript** — the runtime log of turns; unscoped, not a governed
    68	  artifact, never loaded into context.
    69	- **conversation** — a scoped, bounded discussion attached to exactly one
    70	  anchor; every conversation belongs to a process.
    71	- **anchor** — the governed record a conversation attaches to: a session
    72	  record, a review record, or a work item; carries the conversation's
    73	  state across transcript boundaries.
    74	- **conversation type** — discovery (anchor: a session record), review
    75	  (anchor: a review record and its outcomes), or work (anchor: a work
    76	  item).
    77	- **session record** — the anchor record of a discovery conversation:
    78	  outcome, produced and revised lists, open threads, select quotes.
    79	- **review** — one bounded conversation in which the authority decides
    80	  on presented material. Replaces "sitting".
    81	- **review record** — the anchor record of a review: the material
    82	  presented and the outcomes applied; each decision traces in the
    83	  changed artifacts' Document History, never in a standing ledger.
    84	- **park** — the failsafe for work that cannot pass review within its
    85	  round cap: set aside with a filed finding.
    86	- **round cap** — the declared maximum review rounds before a loop's
    87	  failsafe exit fires.
    88	- **action** — the approved choice for one record: keep-rewrite, keep,
    89	  retire, or terminal, with its target.
    90	- **authority-call** — a row marker on a migration-plan row awaiting a
    91	  decision from the authority; not an action — the row has no effect and
    92	  is out of scope for any run until ruled.
    93	- **action table** — the table of records and their actions; approved row
    94	  by row or in blocks at a review. Drives any bulk record change.
    95	- **close-out** — the mechanical execution of the migration plan's
    96	  pre-decided retire and terminal actions: snapshot, delete, archive
    97	  move, a loud post-check, and the branch promotion; no review loop.
    98	  All stages run consecutively at cut-over.
    99	- **snapshot tag** — the annotated git tag (`pre-migration`) on `main`'s
   100	  pre-execution commit, preserving the full corpus for terminal-recovery;
   101	  after close-out a terminal record exists only there.
   102	- **migration plan** — the rebaseline's action table plus the order of
   103	  the per-type migration runs. Replaces "rebaseline bill".
   104	- **run** — one execution of a process, anchored to a work item; states:
   105	  running, held, done, cancelled.
   106	- **hold** — a run state: paused with its step and data preserved in its
   107	  anchor, by inactivity or by an ask; a held run is resumed or
   108	  cancelled, never dropped.
   109	- **checkpoint** — updating a conversation's anchor when a transcript
   110	  ends mid-conversation; a checkpoint is not a close.
   111	- **branched conversation** — a conversation run as a sub-process of
   112	  another run; its anchor records the parent (`branched-from`).
   113	- **Bounded Context** — a deliberately drawn region of the product with
   114	  one internal language, one model, and one set of contracts to other
   115	  regions; drawn by design, never discovered after the fact (Evans,
   116	  *Domain-Driven Design*).
   117	- **shop** — the entity that builds, operates, and evolves part of the
   118	  product. Two types: the lead shop (the system-level coordinator; owns
   119	  product-level artifacts and no Bounded Context) and the BC-shop (owns
   120	  and produces exactly one Bounded Context).
   121	- **activity** — a discrete unit of work performed inside a shop,
   122	  following a contract or process, with inputs, outcomes, and provenance
   123	  recorded. What an actor is stays unconstrained; the activity has the
   124	  same shape whoever performs it.
   125	- **contract** — a named, versioned statement of what one entity
   126	  promises another: the schemas exchanged, the meaning of each
   127	  operation, the error behavior, and the relationship kind. Offered as a
   128	  product contract (on a Bounded Context) or an operational contract (on
   129	  a shop); nothing outside a contract is promised.
   130	- **relationship kind** — the named pattern a contract declares for how
   131	  two Bounded Contexts relate, drawn from the context-mapping catalogue
   132	  (Evans, *Domain-Driven Design*): customer–supplier, conformist,
   133	  anti-corruption layer, open host service, published language, shared
   134	  kernel, or separate ways.
   135	- **vehicle** — the message type a request to a Bounded Context shop
   136	  travels in; chosen from the pre-state, never from the request's
   137	  wording.
   138	- **pre-state** — what a Bounded Context actually is before a change,
   139	  read from its contracts and its scenario register, never from its
   140	  internals.
   141	- **framing** — the PM role's recorded statement of what a request is
   142	  about: the originator, the problem taken to be worth solving, and the
   143	  outcome it serves; the exclusive decision of `lead-pm`.
   144	- **acceptance scenario** — a Gherkin scenario that states, as a
   145	  requirement, what counts as done for one behavior; co-produced by the
   146	  PO role and the owning Bounded Context shop; held in that shop's
   147	  scenario register.
   148	- **scenario register** — a Bounded Context shop's own list of the
   149	  acceptance scenarios it holds, with their hashes and states; read in
   150	  full across every context when a claim spans contexts.
   151	- **ask** — a question one activity puts to another role in place of
   152	  its output, carrying a default and a checkpoint; the run holds, the
   153	  role answers, the step resumes — never a wait in place (data type
   154	  `ask`; process-definition typedef §Run lifecycle).
   155	- **clarify** — an ask from a Bounded Context shop to the lead shop on
   156	  scope, vocabulary, structure, or contract; answered by the role whose
   157	  domain the question falls in.
   158	- **decomposition** — the solutions architect's record of which Bounded
   159	  Context owns which capability, and the relationship kind of each
   160	  contract between contexts.
   161	- **enabler work** — technical work that makes a feature possible;
   162	  recommended by the solutions architect, placed in the backlog by the
   163	  PO role.
   164	- **interaction type** — one way a person or an agent reaches the
   165	  product: command line (CLI), full-screen terminal (TUI), graphical or
   166	  web (GUI), API and SDK, conversational, voice, or a generated document
   167	  or notification; each honours its own conventions within one
   168	  experience.
   169	- **experience guidance corpus** — the product designer role's
   170	  principle set and guidelines for the experience: design principles,
   171	  vocabulary and voice, core-task parity across interaction types,
   172	  interaction patterns per type, and the accessibility target; the
   173	  role's exclusive domain.
   174	- **originator** — whoever expresses intent at the product's edge: a
   175	  person, a shop, or a system outside the product.
   176	- **generated interface** — an interface whose form the product composes
   177	  at run time from the person's context, within constraints the
   178	  experience guidance corpus states; a form, not an interaction type.
   179	- **assistant interaction** — an interaction in which the product acts on
   180	  an intent the person stated rather than on a command — conversational,
   181	  voice, or an assistant acting for the person (a closed set); the locus
   182	  of control is the product's, so the experience principles bound it.
   183	- **intent** — a desired outcome expressed by an originator at the
   184	  product's edge; enters the product through a contract and keeps its
   185	  provenance through every translation and delegation.
